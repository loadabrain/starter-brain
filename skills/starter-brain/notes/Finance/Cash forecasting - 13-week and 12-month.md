# Cash forecasting - 13-week and 12-month

> [!summary] TL;DR
> 13-week: weekly, actual receipts and payments, rolled forward every week.
> 12-month: monthly, for seasonality, tax dates, hiring and buffer planning.
> Direct method (cash in/out by date) beats starting from profit for short horizons.

## Two horizons
| | 13-week | 12-month |
|---|---|---|
| Grain | week | month |
| Question | will I be able to pay next weeks' bills? | what does the year look like, when do I need the buffer? |
| Method | direct: receipts and disbursements by expected payment date | direct, summarised monthly |
| Update | weekly roll: drop the past week, add a new week 13 | monthly or quarterly |

## Rules from practitioners
- Direct method projects actual receipts and payments; the indirect method starts from net income and adjusts for non-cash items and working-capital changes. Most small-company guides recommend direct for short horizons (vendor guides: Beancount, Float, Centime; medium-low trust, they agree).
- Each week: record actuals against last week's forecast, explain variances, roll forward. One missed update makes later weeks wrong.
- Set a minimum cash threshold and act 2-3 weeks before a week breaches it.
- Enter items on the *payment* date, VAT-inclusive where VAT is due (Bpifrance Création, Feb 2025). Add expected customer delay: use your measured DSO (Working capital and the cash conversion cycle), not the invoice terms.
- Provide scenarios (base / late-pay / lost client).

## Steps
1. Copy Template - 13-week cash forecast.
2. Fill opening cash, then receipts by invoice with realistic pay week, then fixed and variable payments, then dated tax items (Corporate advance payments (versements anticipes) - dates, weekend rule and surcharge).
3. Run Checklist - Weekly cash check every week.
4. Once a quarter extend to 12 months and check against [[Burn rate and runway]].

Not financial advice; an accountant should confirm tax dates.
