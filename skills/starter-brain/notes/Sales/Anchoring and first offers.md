# Anchoring and first offers

> [!summary] TL;DR
> Make the first offer when you know the ZOPA, ambitiously but credibly. Costs: more impasses and worse feelings; the effect shrinks as the number of issues grows.

## Key facts
- Anchoring is relying too much on the first number on the table; the first offer pulls counter-offers and the settlement toward it; how strongly depends on how well each side knows the ZOPA [2].
- A preregistered meta-analysis (374 effects, 90 studies, N = 16,334, plus two experiments, N = 2,121; 2025) found a first-mover advantage (g = 0.42) and a strong link between first-offer size and agreement value (r = 0.62) [1].
- Ambitious first offers beat moderate ones (g = 1.14) but caused more impasses (g = −0.42) and lower subjective value for the recipient (g = −0.40), driven by anger [1].
- All these effects get smaller as the number and type of issues grows [1].
- Specific, challenging goals led negotiators to better objective outcomes but lower satisfaction; a public commitment to a goal can cost flexibility and risk impasse (PON, adapted from Schweitzer) [3].

## When to move first (PON's four cases)
1. They know the ZOPA better than you: gather information first.
2. Both know it well: anchors matter little, fairness dominates.
3. Neither knows: you can anchor, but risk going too low or high.
4. You know more: make an ambitious first offer [2].

## How to do it
- Base the anchor on your target and your estimate of their Reservation price, with a short objective justification (Principled negotiation).
- If they anchor first, do not adjust from their number; re-base on your own preparation. A strong BATNA makes rejecting an extreme anchor easier.
- In multi-issue deals use packages (MESO (multiple equivalent simultaneous offers)) and soften the anger risk with Accusation audit and Labeling (the brain's own judgment).

## When it doesn't apply
- Complex multi-issue deals: the anchor matters less [1].
- Ongoing relationships where an angry counterpart costs more than the price gain [1].
