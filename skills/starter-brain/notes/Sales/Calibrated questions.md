# Calibrated questions

> [!summary] TL;DR
> Start with 'what' or 'how', never a yes/no. Use them to say no without saying no, to find constraints, and to plan implementation. Answer 'What makes you ask?' before replying to a question whose purpose you don't know.

## Key facts
- Black Swan defines calibrated questions as open questions starting with "what" or "how"; they make the counterpart work out the answer and shape their thinking [1].
- Uses on the N9 page: break an impasse when they ask for something you cannot deliver ("How am I supposed to do that?") and secure implementation ("How will we know if we are on track?") [1]. Finding constraints and saying 'no' without saying no are the brain's own reading.
- Before answering a question, ask "What makes you ask?" or label: "Seems like you have a reason for asking that" [2].
- To avoid naming a price first, Black Swan pairs a label with "What kind of range do you have in mind?" and mirrors the reply [3]. "How did you arrive at that number?" is the brain's own variant..
- Avoid "why": it can sound accusatory (not stated on the Black Swan pages cited here; the brain's own judgment).

## Script bank
**Push back:** "How am I supposed to do that?" [1] · "What would need to be true for that price to work for both of us?" (the brain's own)
**Implementation:** "How will we know if we're on track?" [1] · "What happens on your side between today and signature?" · "Who else needs to be comfortable with this, and what will they look at?" (the brain's own)

## Details
PON suggests asking for the reasons behind positions; soften it: "Help me understand…" (the brain's own adaptation). Questions make people stop and think, which can slow them opening up; mix with Labeling (Black Swan) [1].

## When it doesn't apply
- Black Swan's pages are vendor material with no controlled test; effects across cultures are unproven (Cross-cultural negotiation in Europe).
- Don't stack many in a row; it reads as interrogation (the brain's own judgment).
